Buyers Are the Bottleneck

An operating plan for Foreguild, written from the numbers of the closest AI job board already running: 394 on-chain bounties, 525 AI-jury verdicts, 61 external buyers.

Non-internal entry by drdz23 (David Rodríguez, Honduras) · November 2026 · Drafted with an AI assistant (Claude), every number recomputed from public data · Disclosure: I am an active hunter on Verdikta Bounties, the board analysed below (second by number of awards in this dataset). I have no affiliation with Seer or Verdikta.

Thesis. Agent labour is already abundant: on the closest live market, the winning answer arrives a median 13 minutes after a task opens, and 67 different agents shared 240 awards. What is scarce is an outside buyer who pays for a well-specified outcome and comes back. On that market the platform itself posted 51% of all tasks, and 50 of its 61 outside "buyers" were also workers recycling their winnings. Foreguild should therefore spend its agents, its treasury and its futarchy on one thing: turning outside buyers into repeat buyers, and measure itself only by the fees those buyers pay.

1. What a live AI job board actually looks like

Foreguild's core loop is "humans and AIs post tasks, AI completes them and gets paid in crypto". That loop already runs on Verdikta Bounties (Base): a creator escrows ETH with a published rubric, agents submit, a multi-model AI jury scores each submission, and the first submission that clears the threshold is paid. I pulled every job from its public API (/api/jobs/<id>, ids 1–469, deduplicated, 12 Sep – 6 Nov 2026) and joined the rubric of each bounty from IPFS. ETH is converted at $2,233.

Supply is cheap and fast

The median bounty received 1 submission (mean 1.59), and it was usually enough: 76% of awards went to the first submission. 67 distinct wallets won at least once, but the top two took 35% of awards. Workers are not the constraint. Competition between them is about speed, and speed is already near zero.

Two conclusions shape everything below. First, the whole outside market was about $1,360 in eight weeks; a 10% fee on it would have earned about $136. A plan that simply copies the loop and adds a fee does not produce a business. Second, "volume" is easy to fake: most outside posting came from workers re-posting winnings, and the platform's own wallets created half the board. Any revenue metric that counts this activity will reward the wrong behaviour, and a futarchy that optimises it will be gamed.

Why tasks fail: the specification, not the worker

I ran a rubric linter on every bounty the platform posted itself (185 with a public rubric) and back-tested it against outcomes, using only properties known at launch.

12 of the 13 bounties that expired with outside submissions and no winner had a defect detectable before launch. The single costliest one was the threshold: 90 or above passed 15% of submissions, 85–89 passed 58%. A series of seven bounties at 92–94 received 23 scored submissions and paid nobody. Five of the failures had a best score within 1–5 points of a 92–94 bar and would all have paid at 89. A buyer whose task fails for reasons like these does not come back.

Caveat: the linter's thresholds were calibrated on this same history, so the back-test is a consistency check. Section 7 lists out-of-sample predictions that Foreguild's first markets would test.

The cost of a verdict

Every jury evaluation consumes an oracle prepay of about 0.00024 ETH ($0.54). The mean bounty was 0.0029 ETH ($6.50), and it took 1.59 evaluations on average. Evaluation alone is about 13% of the value of an average task, more than a typical marketplace fee. On a $5 task, nothing is left for the guild. Revenue needs bigger tasks, not more tiny ones.

2. What Foreguild sells

Foreguild does not sell agent labour; that is a commodity. It sells specifications that clear: a task written so that it gets done, judged fairly and paid on the first attempt. Its customer is the buyer. Revenue comes from four lines, ordered by how early they can earn.

4. How futarchy directs the agents

The welfare metric: Net Repeat Buyer Revenue (NRBR-90)

Foreguild maximises one number: fees retained over the next 90 days from net outside buyers who pay at least twice.

  • Retained: counted only after the appeal window closes, net of refunds.
  • Net outside buyer: a wallet cluster that is not Foreguild, its treasury or its agents, and that paid more into tasks in the window than it received as a worker. A worker re-posting winnings nets to zero and adds nothing. This is the lesson of the 50 of 61 Verdikta creators who were also workers.
  • Repeat: the buyer funded two or more accepted tasks in the window. Line A clients count when they pay twice.

The metric is computed by an open script over on-chain settlement events and resolved through Seer's oracle (Reality.eth) with the script and data attached, so anyone can re-run it and dispute a wrong answer.

The decision loop

  1. The Proposer agent or any token-free participant posts a FIP: one parameter change or one budget, with evidence.
  2. Seer opens a decision market ("Is FIP-n adopted?") with two conditional scalar markets on NRBR-90: one if adopted, one if not. The branch that does not happen is voided, and traders in it get their collateral back.
  3. The Treasurer seeds both branches with the same small liquidity budget, so a thin market is not automatically one-sided.
  4. After 7 days, the FIP is adopted if the time-weighted price over the last 72 hours of "NRBR-90 if adopted" beats "if not adopted" by at least 3%. Below the minimum liquidity, or inside the margin, the status quo wins.
  5. The adopted change is executed by the agent that owns that parameter. 90 days later the markets resolve on the measured metric, which pays the traders who priced it right.

What futarchy controls, and what it does not

Humans keep exactly one power: a 2-of-3 guardian key can pause the board for a safety or legal emergency, publishing the reason within 24 hours. It cannot propose, spend or change parameters. Every pause automatically becomes a FIP ("resume?") on the next cycle.

5. The first four proposals

Futarchy is only useful if it has real questions to price. These are the first four, each with the evidence a trader would use.

FIP-1: Cap thresholds at 89 for writing and research tasks

Evidence: 90+ passed 15% of submissions vs 58% at 85–89; seven bounties at 92–94 paid 0 of 23; five failed bounties had best scores within 1–5 points and would have paid at 89. A buyer whose task pays on the first try is a buyer who returns. Expected effect: more first-try clears and a higher repeat rate, at a small cost in average quality.

FIP-2: Minimum task size $20

Evidence: the mean task was $6.50, and evaluation alone consumed about 13% of it. Tiny tasks attract tests, promotions and recycled winnings rather than buyers. The market prices whether losing those tasks costs more repeat revenue than it saves.

FIP-3: Free first evaluation for new outside buyers

The treasury pays the evaluation prepay on the first two tasks of a new net outside buyer (about $1.10 per buyer). This is the cheapest acquisition lever available. It is only worth it if those buyers come back, which is exactly what NRBR-90 measures.

FIP-4: Best-at-deadline for tasks above $100

Evidence: in a first-passing-wins race, 76% of awards went to the first submission and the median winner arrived in 13 minutes. That is ideal for checkable micro-tasks and bad for work where quality varies. The market decides where the cut-off should sit.

6. Disputes, spending and human intervention

  • Appeals. Either side can appeal a verdict within 72 hours by posting a bond of twice the evaluation cost. A second jury with a different model mix re-scores the same evidence. If the verdict flips, the bond is returned and escrow follows the new verdict; otherwise the bond pays the second jury. Escrow is held through the window, so nothing has to be clawed back. On Verdikta, 30% of rejected scored submissions landed between 40 and 60, where one juror's gate decision splits the panel. That is exactly the band where a second opinion is worth paying for.
  • Spending. Operating costs (compute, gas, oracle fees) run on a monthly budget approved by futarchy. Anything above $500 outside the budget is its own FIP.
  • Human intervention. It is limited to the pause key described above and to reviewing content flagged for legal risk. Every intervention is logged and becomes a market question.

Ten predictions, checkable at day 90

Written now, so Seer can grade this plan against what happens.

  1. At least 70% of tasks that pass Preflight with no high-severity defect are paid on the first window.
  2. Tasks with a high-severity defect that the buyer overrides pay at least 20 points less often.
  3. Under FIP-1, first-try clears for writing tasks rise by at least 15 points over the 92–94 baseline.
  4. Median time to an accepted submission stays under 30 minutes for open-race tasks under $50.
  5. Buyers whose first task paid on the first try return at least twice as often as buyers whose first task failed.
  6. Recycled-winnings wallets account for under 10% of NRBR-90-eligible fees once netting is applied.
  7. Line A reaches at least 300 paid evaluations in month 3.
  8. Appeals flip at most 15% of contested verdicts.
  9. No more than 5% of funded tasks are orphaned or refunded for platform reasons.
  10. The decision markets reject at least one of FIP-1 to FIP-4. A futarchy that approves everything is not measuring anything.

9. Risks

  • Buyers never arrive. This is the main risk, and the reason line A exists: it earns from other boards' buyers before Foreguild has its own.
  • Thin markets. Low liquidity defaults to the status quo, and the Treasurer seeds both branches equally. Futarchy is used for a few consequential decisions per quarter, not for every parameter.
  • Metric gaming. Netting removes recycled winnings, the Auditor clusters wallets, and the metric can only be changed by a delayed FIP.
  • Jury error. At least two providers, appeals with a different model mix, and published verdicts. AI juries are not infallible, and the appeal bond prices that honestly.
  • Race-to-the-bottom quality. Open races are reserved for checkable tasks; FIP-4 lets the market move the cut-off.
